About the dataset

The Bark and Ambrosia Beetle Pathogen Dataset is a literature-derived resource compiling reported pathogens and parasites associated with bark and ambrosia beetles (Scolytinae). Version 1.0 contains 1,015 records extracted from 281 primary publications published between 1914 and 2026, representing 108 beetle species in 28 genera and 274 unique candidate pathogen or parasite taxa.

The dataset includes fungi, bacteria, viruses, nematodes, protists, and microsporidia, together with information on beetle hosts, geographic locations, infection sites, organism sources, identification methods, reported ecological relationships, experimental conditions, and evidence of infection, pathogenicity, or virulence.

Scope and interpretation

A record in this dataset represents a literature-derived pathogen- or parasite–beetle association reported in a specific publication and geographic context. Records should not be interpreted as independent biological observations or as estimates of natural pathogen prevalence.

Importantly, inclusion in the dataset does not necessarily indicate experimentally demonstrated pathogenicity. Some records document organisms reported as pathogens or parasites by the original authors, whereas others are supported by experimental infection, pathogenicity, or virulence tests. Evidence type and validation information are retained separately to allow users to distinguish reported associations from experimentally supported interactions.

Taxonomic names were reconciled to current accepted nomenclature where possible. Such standardization represents nomenclatural reconciliation rather than re-identification of the original biological material.

Dataset overview

Fungi were the most frequently reported group, with 408 records across 59 taxa, while nematodes comprised the greatest taxonomic diversity with 121 unique taxa across 221 records. Viruses were the least represented category (18 taxa from 47 records). Only 245 records (24.14%) reported any experimental validation of infection, pathogenicity, or virulence.

Experimental validation & evidence types

Inclusion in the dataset does not necessarily indicate experimentally demonstrated pathogenicity. Some records document organisms reported as pathogens or parasites by the original authors based on natural surveys, whereas 245 records (24.14%) are supported by formal infection, pathogenicity, or virulence tests.
